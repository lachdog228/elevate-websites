Most small business websites are launched and then quietly abandoned. Nobody notices anything is wrong until a customer mentions the contact form has been broken for a month, or the site goes down on the busiest weekend of the year.

Maintenance exists to catch those things before your customers do.

What's covered

  • Security patching — dependencies and server software kept current
  • Off-site backups — taken regularly and stored separately, so a server failure doesn't take your backups with it
  • Uptime monitoring — the site is pinged continuously and I'm alerted on failure, usually before you'd notice
  • Broken link checks — dead internal links frustrate visitors and waste crawl budget
  • Core Web Vitals monitoring — performance degrades gradually as content is added; this catches it
  • Content updates — a set allowance each month for text changes, new photos, updated hours or pricing
  • Form testing — enquiry forms verified regularly, because a silently broken form is the most expensive fault a small site can have

Why forms deserve their own line

A contact form that stops delivering email is invisible from the outside. The page still loads, the form still submits, the success message still appears — and the enquiries simply never arrive. Businesses routinely lose weeks of leads this way and conclude their website doesn't work. Regular testing is a small amount of effort against a genuinely costly failure.

What you get each month

A short plain-English note covering what was done, anything that needed fixing, and how the site is performing. Not a forty-page automated PDF — just enough to know your money is doing something.

You might not need this. If your site is static, custom-built and rarely changes, the real risk is low and ad-hoc support may serve you better. Maintenance earns its cost when you have a CMS, bookings, payments, or you just want someone watching so you're not the last to know something broke.
